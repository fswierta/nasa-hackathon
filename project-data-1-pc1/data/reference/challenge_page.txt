Flame in Freefall: SI-Powered Fire Safety Insights from Microgravity Combustion Data
Event: 2026 NASA Space Apps Challenge | Difficulty: Advanced, Intermediate | Subject: Space Exploration
=====================================================================

SUMMARY
For decades, NASA has studied how flames and fire behave in microgravity, gathering a trove of
experimental data along the way. That knowledge matters more than ever as humans gear up to return
to the Moon and press on to Mars, yet the volume of results makes them hard to find, compare, and
understand. Your challenge is to create an interactive, super intelligence (SI) powered dashboard
that summarizes, ranks, and interprets those findings to deliver fire safety insights for human
space exploration.

BACKGROUND
Fire hazards are extremely dangerous in space exploration! In describing a smoke alarm that went off
during the recent Artemis II mission, the commander commented, "[If] you want to get somebody's
attention really quick, make the fire alarm go off in your spacecraft when you're still about 80,000
miles from home." Decades earlier, the Apollo 13 crew endured the life-threatening results of an
oxygen tank explosion on their way to the Moon. Similarly, a multinational crew survived a fire
aboard the Mir space station caused by an oxygen generator failure. In 1967, the Apollo 1 crew died
in a pre-launch test when fire erupted in their oxygen-filled spacecraft.

Compared to Earth, flames in free fall (i.e., in microgravity) are often dramatically different
because of the absence of buoyancy and thus rising hot gases. Instead of a flickering teardrop, a
candle flame becomes a ghostly blue sphere reliant on diffusion for its oxygen.

NASA's spacecraft fire safety work is focused on prevention, detection, and suppression. Prevention
is achieved by controlling ignition sources and through material selection, which depends on
understanding flammability limits. To facilitate spacesuit use, an atmosphere with increased oxygen
and reduced pressure compared to Earth's is expected within the planned Moon Base and pressurized
rovers. Flammability is highly dependent on oxygen concentration, and air flow is also important in
microgravity. Flammability studies focus on solid materials such as fabrics and plastics, as studied
in the Burning and Suppression of Solids (BASS and BASS-II) and Spacecraft Fire Safety Demonstration
(Saffire-I, -II, and -III) investigations.

Fire detection was tested in microgravity through the Smoke and Aerosol Measurement Experiment
studies (SAME and SAME-R). Suppression was studied in the Flame Extinguishment Experiment (FLEX)
through dilution of the chamber atmosphere with carbon dioxide and helium, both of which suppress fire.

OBJECTIVES
Results from low-gravity combustion experiments, conducted for example on the International Space
Station (ISS), are being made publicly available through NASA's Physical Science Informatics (PSI)
database, but the amount of data can be overwhelming. It can be hard to find, harder to compare, and
harder yet to interpret.

Your challenge is to build an SI-powered, web-based dashboard that summarizes, ranks, and interprets
combustion science investigations to deliver critical foundational insights for fire safety. You may
consider enabling users to:
 - Identify investigations most relevant to the fire safety needs of habitable crew environments,
   including spacecraft, space stations, bases on the lunar and Martian surfaces, and pressurized rovers.*
 - Extract data that supports predictive modeling of flammability and fire scenarios in space exploration.
 - Highlight research that informs the development of next-generation fire safety controls and equipment.

In this challenge, you'll help turn complex data into clear answers and make the next giant leap a
safer one.

Your dashboard could also address potential applications of the PSI data to fire safety on Earth.
Fine-water-mist extinguishers developed for use on the ISS have practical applications here on
Earth, and NASA technology has similarly led to improvements in terrestrial smoke detection and
firefighter gear, including protective clothing and air tanks.

*Fire safety involving rockets and propellants is generally beyond the scope of the research
available in the PSI database. The high velocities within rocket engines render the combustion
effectively independent of gravity - although a rocket's launch is of course strongly dependent on
gravity.

POTENTIAL CONSIDERATIONS (optional)
- Target audience can include professors, graduate students, undergraduate students, aerospace
  researchers, and aerospace engineers.
- Consider how you might summarize and display attributes such as:
    Experimental objectives
    Fuels, including shape and size of the samples or burners
    Other test conditions, such as oxygen concentration and airflow speed
    Variables: how did tests from the same investigation differ and how were they the same?
    Sensors or types of measurements
- Consider how to summarize and display similarities and differences between different investigations.
- Other investigations in PSI:
    One of the instruments used for smoke detection in SAME and SAME-R was instead used to detect
    dust in the Dust and Aerosol Measurement Feasibility Test (DAFT and DAFT-2) studies.
    While it burned gaseous fuels, the Burning Rate Emulator (BRE) experiment of the ACME project
    was a study of materials flammability in still atmospheres as a function of material properties.
    Other combustion investigations within PSI (not explicitly referenced above) are still relevant
    to fire safety, just not as strongly or directly.
- Hardware:
    The six experiments in the ACME project featured flames of gaseous fuels and were all conducted
    with the same modular set of ACME hardware in the Combustion Integrated Rack (CIR).
    FLEX, FLEX-2, and the Cool Flames Investigation (CFI) each featured burning droplets of liquid
    fuel and were conducted with the Multi-user Droplet Combustion Apparatus (MDCA) in the CIR.
    BASS, BASS-II, SPICE, and SLICE collectively featured both solid and gaseous fuels. Each was
    conducted within the Microgravity Science Glovebox (MSG) using the modular fan-driven flow duct
    built for SPICE.
- Spacesuits and oxygen concentration:
    Spacesuits are filled with 100% oxygen to only 4.3 psi; Earth sea-level pressure is 14.7 psi.
    To avoid the bends, astronauts pre-breathe 100% oxygen to expel absorbed nitrogen.
    To reduce pre-breathing and facilitate lunar excursions, the Moon Base and pressurized rover(s)
    are expected to be at a pressure and O2 concentration between a spacesuit and Earth's atmosphere.
    The intermediate oxygen concentration used on the Moon will likely be 30% or greater, with a
    corresponding pressure of 10.2 psi or less - more than a 40% increase in O2 concentration vs.
    Earth's atmosphere, making materials much more flammable.
    Microgravity tests with the highest O2 concentrations were in ACME CFI-G: chamber filled with
    N2 + flammable gas (e.g., propane) while an O2/N2 mixture of up to 85% O2 flowed from the
    spherical burner creating an inside-out (inverse) flame.
- Partial-gravity data:
    There is currently no partial-gravity combustion data in PSI. Such data is expected in the future
    (e.g., Lunar-g Combustion Investigation (LUCI), Flammability of Materials on the Moon (FM2)).
    Partial gravity was mimicked in ACME E-FIELD Flames through the effect of the electric field on
    ions naturally occurring in flames.
    Microgravity and normal-gravity results could bound what occurs at intermediate gravity levels.
